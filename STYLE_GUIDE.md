# John Mansoor — Writing Style Guide

This file is reference context for any writing project: the book *After Work*,
LinkedIn posts, Substack essays, query letters, and the social-presence agent.
It has two halves:

1. **My voice** — what my writing should sound like.
2. **Anti-AI tells** — machine fingerprints to strip out, adapted from the
   Wikipedia field guide on detecting AI-generated text.

Both matter. Sounding like me and not sounding like a machine are different
targets, so they get separate checklists.

---

## PART 1 — MY VOICE

The positive target. This is drawn from my actual writing (the *After Work*
preface) and refined across the project.

### What my writing sounds like
- Direct and clinically precise, with dry humor.
- Vulnerable without self-pity. I can say a hard thing about myself without
  asking for sympathy.
- I write from the body and from the room — a specific person in a specific
  chair, a pen rolling between palms, the traffic outside the window.
- I build tension slowly and let it sit. I don't rush to resolve it.
- I balance two registers: the clinical and the personal. Neither one wins.
- I'm writing toward hard questions, not from a safe distance on the far side
  of them.

### How a piece should open
- Start with a specific moment, image, or clinical detail. Never an abstraction,
  never a thesis statement, never a question.
- Put a body in the scene early. A patient, my brother, me.

### How a piece should move
- Short sentences when a point lands. Let them be blunt.
- Name the feeling before I explain the theory behind it.
- Use real clinical observation — composite, anonymized patients — over generic
  advice.
- One idea at a time. I don't stack three claims when one carries the weight.

### How a piece should end
- End on something unresolved, or a quiet observation, or an honest "I didn't
  have a good answer." Not a neat bow, not a lesson, not a call to action.
- The last line should land, not summarize.

### My hard nos (voice)
- No starting on a question.
- No "Here's the thing," "Let me be clear," "Hot take."
- No motivational-poster sentences.
- No "journey." No "leverage," "unlock," "unpack," "navigate," "lean in."
- No "the future of work" or "in this economy" as filler.
- No lists longer than five items.
- No emoji in body text (a Substack Note can be an exception).

## PART 2 — ANTI-AI TELLS

The negative target. Strip these out. Adapted from the Wikipedia guide to
detecting AI writing; trimmed to what applies to my non-encyclopedia work.

### Banned word: "quietly"
Do not use "quietly" (or "quiet" as a filler intensifier: "a quiet migration,"
"quietly doing the work"). It has become a personal tell from overuse. Cut it
or replace it with something concrete.

### Undue significance / legacy puffery
Never write that something "stands as a testament," "serves as a reminder,"
"plays a vital/pivotal/crucial role," "marks a turning point," "reflects a
broader," "leaves an indelible mark," or "underscores its importance." Say the
thing plainly instead.

### Superficial "-ing" tag-ons
Don't end sentences with editorializing participle phrases: "...highlighting the
impact," "...reflecting a broader shift," "...emphasizing the importance,"
"...showcasing its significance." Stop when the thought stops.

### Promotional / peacock words
Avoid: vibrant, rich (figurative), profound, boasts, nestled, in the heart of,
groundbreaking, renowned, seamless, testament, diverse array, breathtaking,
commitment to.

### Vague attributions
No "observers note," "experts argue," "many believe," "studies show,"
"it is widely recognized." A claim gets a named source or it's my own
observation.

### Negative parallelisms
Avoid the AI cadence: "not just X, but Y," "it isn't about X, it's about Y,"
"no X, no Y, just Z." This is the single most overused machine rhythm. Use it
almost never, and only when I genuinely mean it.

### Rule-of-three padding
Don't reach for three adjectives or three parallel phrases to sound complete.
Three real things are fine ("structure, competence, belonging"). Three filler
words to pad a sentence are not ("engaging, impactful, meaningful").

### AI vocabulary
Avoid: delve, intricate, multifaceted, tapestry, landscape (figurative), realm,
foster, garner, bolster, underscore, pivotal, crucial, robust, nuanced,
holistic, harness, showcase, enduring, vibrant.

### Copula avoidance
Plain "is" and "are" are good. Don't swap them for "serves as," "stands as,"
"represents," "features," "offers" to sound elevated.

### Challenge/future-prospect wrap-ups
Don't close by gesturing at challenges ahead or hopeful possibilities. End on
the concrete.

### Essay-summary closers
No "In conclusion," "Overall," "Ultimately," "At the end of the day."

### False ranges
Don't use "from X to Y" unless there's a real scale between them. "From the Big
Bang to human creativity" is not a range; it's filler.

### Punctuation and formatting tells
- Em dashes: rare. No more than one per short piece. A comma or period usually
  does the job.
- Use straight quotes and apostrophes, never curly.
- No title-case headings. No mechanical boldface on every key term.
- No "key takeaways" bullet lists with bold inline headers.

### The test
Would a skilled human writer who knows me actually write this sentence? If it
smells like filler built to sound impressive, cut it.
